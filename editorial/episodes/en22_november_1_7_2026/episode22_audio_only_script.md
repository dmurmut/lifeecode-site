## Under the Microscope

On the sixth of November, Connecticut's attorney general, joined by Michigan and New Jersey, announced a settlement with five Perry Johnson companies. New York announced it too. These companies did coding, billing, and recording work for health-care organizations. Think of the person who turns a doctor's dictated notes into a written record. That kind of work.

Now, this is a settlement with state attorneys general. It isn't a court finding. But here is what the attorneys general say happened.

In March 2023, hackers logged in using an employee's credentials. The company did not require multi-factor authentication for remote access. That's the second check — the code sent to your phone — that many of us use for email. Once inside, the attackers harvested passwords and opened files that were not encrypted.

And here's the part that stays with me. The company's own software recorded the intruders. It saw them. But it wasn't set up to alert anyone. The companies only found out two months later — when the hackers contacted them.

What was in those files? Names. Dates of birth. Addresses. Social Security numbers. Diagnoses. Insurance details. Lab and diagnostic results. Medication names. More than thirteen point three million people nationwide. About four point two million in New York alone.

The money is modest up front: at least three hundred thousand dollars now. Another nine point seven million is due only if the companies fail to keep the terms. And those terms are the real story — a full security program, multi-factor authentication, encryption, annual risk assessments, and rules for how long health data is kept and how it's destroyed.

So what's the hidden risk? Distance. You choose your hospital. You don't choose the vendor behind it. Yet that vendor may hold the most detailed version of your story. And none of the failures described here were exotic. A missing login check. Unencrypted files. An alarm that never rang. These are basics.

One fair caution: this case doesn't prove that other transcription companies have the same gaps. The lesson is about oversight — and about the questions you're allowed to ask.

So, what can you do? Read breach letters carefully, especially if they name a company you don't recognize. If your Social Security number was involved, think about a credit freeze or the free monitoring offered. Ask your provider which outside companies handle your records, and whether they use multi-factor authentication and encryption. And glance at your insurance statements for services you never received.

The bigger picture? Over the next five to ten years, more of your records will pass through vendors — including AI note-takers. Vendor security is becoming patient safety. The question won't just be "is my hospital secure?" It'll be "who else is holding my story?"

Our Privacy Rating for this one: High.

## This Week's Signals

**Signal two. A smartwatch and your blood pressure.**

On the fifth of November, Google Research — working with Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and Harvard Medical School — described a study published in Nature. Their model used a consumer smartwatch's pulse sensor and motion data, plus a person's height, to look for signs of high blood pressure.

In a research study of one hundred ninety-six people at five US sites, checked against twenty-four-hour blood-pressure monitoring, the watch model caught about sixty-six percent of people who had high blood pressure, and correctly cleared about ninety percent of people who didn't. A single office check caught about fifty-five percent.

That sounds impressive. But Google itself calls this early feasibility research. It's not a product feature, and it's not a diagnosis. Google's current blood-pressure trends feature is labelled for general wellness — and "not a prescreener" for hypertension.

Why does it matter? High blood pressure often has no symptoms. A gentle nudge to get a proper cuff reading could be useful one day. Today, no watch replaces that cuff, or your clinician.

**Signal three. Robots that act on their own — or from far away.**

Also on the sixth, the FDA posted a discussion paper asking how to weigh benefits and risks for medical robots that work with some autonomy, or are steered remotely. It's a request for feedback, open until the fifth of January. The FDA is clear: this is not guidance, draft or final.

For you, the question is simple. If software or a surgeon in another city is involved in a procedure, who's in control at each step — and what happens if the connection drops?

**Signal four. Hospital websites and Meta.**

In an order dated the thirtieth of October — two days before our window, but reported during it — a federal judge in California certified four classes of Facebook users in the Meta Pixel health-care case. Plaintiffs allege that a tracking tool on more than six hundred sixty hospital and provider websites sent health information — clicks, conditions, doctors, prescriptions — to Meta without consent.

Let's be precise. This ruling lets people sue together. It does not decide whether Meta broke any law, and Meta plans to appeal. But it asks a question worth sitting with: when you searched a hospital website, was that private?

**Signal five. India's AI scribe that waits for a doctor.**

In Kolkata, on the fifth and sixth of November, India's National Health Authority demonstrated an AI voice scribe. It turns a doctor–patient conversation into a structured record, in several Indian languages. And here's the safeguard: a doctor has to review and approve that record before it's linked to the patient's health account.

States were also encouraged to benchmark AI tools before buying them, and to name dedicated cybersecurity leads. These were demonstrations and recommendations — no rollout dates, no accuracy figures yet. Still, human sign-off built in from the start is exactly the kind of design to look for.

## Privacy Radar

Time for the Privacy Radar.

The Perry Johnson breach: High. Your data sat with vendors you never chose. Read breach notices, consider a credit freeze, and ask which vendors handle your records.

The smartwatch blood-pressure research: Medium. Treat any alert as a prompt for a proper cuff reading, and check your health app's sharing settings.

The FDA robotics paper: Low for now. Before a robot-assisted procedure, ask who controls each step and what happens if the link fails.

The Meta Pixel case: High. Use tracking protection on health websites, and use the patient portal or the phone for sensitive bookings.

India's AI scribe: Medium. Ask whether AI helped write your notes, and check your record for mistakes.
