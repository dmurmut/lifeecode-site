## Under the Microscope

On the sixteenth of November, the US Department of Health and Human Services announced a final rule updating the HIPAA Privacy Rule. That's the federal rule that governs how doctors, hospitals, health plans, and their vendors handle your health information.

First, the status — because it matters. This rule is final, but it is not in force yet. It takes effect sixty days after publication in the Federal Register, and organizations then have another hundred and eighty days to comply. Legal analysts put those dates at January nineteenth and July nineteenth, 2027. So until next summer, the old rules still apply.

What changes for you?

One: speed. When you ask for your records, providers and plans will have fifteen calendar days to respond, down from thirty — with one extension of up to thirty days.

Two: your app. The rule clarifies when a provider must send your electronic records through a personal health app you choose — as long as the provider can produce them in that form. You can also send a copy to a third party you pick.

Three: fees. Providers will have to post estimated fee schedules on their websites, tell you about fees in advance, and in some cases provide electronic access free.

Four: fewer hoops. The rule eases identity checks for people asking for their own records. And you'll no longer be asked to sign a form saying you received the privacy notice.

And five — this one deserves a careful look — the rule expressly allows providers and plans to share health information with social-services agencies and community organizations to coordinate an individual's care.

So far, that sounds like good news. And much of it is. Faster access and clear fees make it easier to see your own history, take it to a new doctor, or catch a mistake.

Now, the hidden risk. Take a breath here, because this is the heart of it.

HIPAA protects your records while a covered provider, plan, or their vendor holds them. But once you send your records into a consumer app that isn't covered by HIPAA, HIPAA no longer protects that copy. What protects it then is the app's own privacy policy, the Federal Trade Commission's general consumer-protection powers and its breach-notification rule, and whatever your state's law says.

That backstop is thinner. In September, the FTC withdrew its 2021 policy statement explaining how its breach rule applies to health apps — though the rule itself still stands. And as we saw in our last episode, outside HIPAA, a company's privacy promise is often the main protection you have.

As for the care-coordination change: helping a clinic connect you with housing or food support can genuinely improve health. It's also sharing that doesn't need your separate permission. Both things are true.

So what can you do?

Know the timeline: from July 2027, expect your records within fifteen days. Before you connect records to an app, read its privacy policy — does it share or sell data, use it for ads or AI training, and can you delete it? Prefer apps that let you disconnect and leave. And remember, connecting is optional. You can always ask for a copy directly.

Looking five to ten years out, more of your health record will live on your phone. That can make you a stronger partner in your care. But US law still protects data based on who holds it — not what it is. Until that changes, the same lab result can be well protected at the hospital and lightly protected one tap later.

## This Week's Signals

Now, four signals.

Signal two: AI-drafted hospital discharge summaries.

A peer-reviewed study in JAMA Network Open, published on the seventeenth, looked at nearly thirteen hundred discharge summaries from five London NHS hospitals — about half written by doctors, half drafted by an AI model running in a secure environment. Twelve resident doctors reviewed them.

After adjustment, reviewers signed off the AI drafts with no or only minor edits about twenty-two percentage points more often than the doctors' own. They also rated potential harm less often in the AI drafts.

But hold on to the limits. This was a retrospective study, one AI model, resident reviewers only, and no patient outcomes. And doctors still checked every summary. The lesson isn't "AI writes better than doctors." It's that a clinician's sign-off remains the safeguard.

Signal three: chatbots and emotional support.

Another research letter in JAMA Network Open, from Japan, surveyed nearly sixty-nine hundred adults who didn't have a usual source of care. Among people with lower mental-health need, under five percent regularly used AI for emotional support. Among those with the highest need, it was about one in five.

That's an association, from self-reported survey data. The study didn't look at what the chatbots said, or whether they helped or harmed. The authors say it may signal unmet need.

And if you're someone who turns to a chatbot when things are hard — you're not alone. But a chatbot isn't a clinician. Please reach out to a qualified professional or a local support line.

Signal four: a clinic group's breach.

In a filing with the US Securities and Exchange Commission on the eighteenth, SBC Medical Group — whose network mainly serves cosmetic and aesthetic clinics in Japan — disclosed unauthorized access detected on the sixteenth. The company said basic personal information on about one point six million people was affected — names, contact details, passwords, birth dates, patient card numbers — plus booking requests, reviews, and records held through a third-party booking platform.

The company says it has not confirmed that electronic medical records were taken. And those categories overlap, so don't add them up.

The lesson: a booking history alone can reveal what treatment someone sought. If you've used a clinic's booking site, change any reused passwords and be wary of messages that quote your appointment details.

Signal five: wearables and exercise.

A peer-reviewed analysis of a large US national survey, from the University of Mississippi, found that about one in three people who mainly used wearables, or several digital tools, met both the aerobic and strength guidelines — compared with about one in five who mostly used patient portals or engaged little.

The researchers are clear: this doesn't prove the devices cause more activity. Active people may simply be more likely to buy them. A tracker can be a nudge — the habit is what counts.

There's no standalone Global Watch this week. The strongest international candidate, a new UK women's-health evidence programme, doesn't yet touch digital tools, so we chose not to stretch it. Our international signals this week came from Japan, inside the main stories.

## Privacy Radar

Time for the Privacy Radar.

The HIPAA rule: medium. Your providers are covered — but an app you send records to usually isn't. Read its privacy policy first.

AI discharge summaries: low. The AI ran inside the hospital's secure environment. Ask who reviewed your summary.

Emotional-support chatbots: medium. The chatbot provider holds your conversations. Avoid identifying details, and check your chat-history settings.

The SBC breach: high. Change reused passwords and watch for messages that use clinic details.

Wearables and exercise: low. Review what your wearable app shares.
