## 1:05–5:00 — Under the Microscope

### Segment A — What happened (1:05–2:35)

**NARRATION**
On the sixteenth of November, the US Department of Health and Human Services announced a final rule updating the HIPAA Privacy Rule — the rule governing how doctors, hospitals, health plans, and their vendors handle your health information.

Status first. It's final, but not in force yet. It takes effect sixty days after Federal Register publication, with another hundred and eighty days to comply — legal analysts put that at January and July 2027.

What changes? Fifteen days to answer your records request, down from thirty, with one extension of up to thirty days. A clearer duty to send your electronic records to a personal health app you choose, when the provider can produce them that way. Posted fee schedules and advance notice of fees. Easier identity checks. No more signing a form to acknowledge the privacy notice. And express permission to share information with social-services and community organizations to coordinate an individual's care.

### Segment B — The hidden risk / the opportunity (2:45–3:55)

**NARRATION**
The opportunity is real: faster access and clear fees make it easier to see your history, take it to a new doctor, or catch a mistake.

Now the hidden risk. HIPAA protects your records while a covered provider, plan, or vendor holds them. Once you send them into a consumer app that isn't covered by HIPAA, HIPAA no longer protects that copy. What's left is the app's privacy policy, the FTC's consumer-protection powers and breach-notification rule, and state law. And in September, the FTC withdrew its 2021 statement on how that breach rule applies to health apps — though the rule itself stands.

The care-coordination change cuts both ways: helping a clinic connect you to housing or food support can improve health. It's also sharing without your separate permission.

## 5:00–8:40 — This Week's Signals

### Signal 2 — AI discharge summaries (5:00–6:00)

**NARRATION**
A peer-reviewed study in JAMA Network Open compared nearly thirteen hundred discharge summaries from five London NHS hospitals — half by doctors, half drafted by an AI model in a secure environment. After adjustment, reviewers signed off the AI drafts with no or minor edits about twenty-two percentage points more often, and rated potential harm less often. But it was retrospective, one model, resident reviewers only, no patient outcomes — and doctors checked every one. The safeguard is still the sign-off.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT:]** Peer-reviewed · retrospective · 5 NHS hospitals · +22 pts signed off (adjusted) · No patient-outcome data

### Signal 3 — Emotional-support chatbots (6:00–6:55)

**NARRATION**
A research letter in JAMA Network Open, from Japan, surveyed nearly sixty-nine hundred adults without a usual source of care. Regular emotional-support AI use rose from under five percent among people with lower mental-health need to about one in five among those with the highest need. That's an association from self-reported data; the study didn't assess what the chatbots said or whether they helped. If you're struggling, please reach a qualified professional or a local support line.

**[VISUAL CUE:]** Calm, low-intensity bar chart in teal rising across four groups; soft speech-bubble outlines in the background. No faces, no distress imagery.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT:]** 4.6% → 20.7% · Association only · Self-reported · Japan

### Signal 4 — SBC Medical Group breach (6:55–7:50)

**NARRATION**
In an SEC filing on the eighteenth, SBC Medical Group — whose network mainly serves cosmetic clinics in Japan — said unauthorized access detected on the sixteenth affected basic personal information on about one point six million people, plus booking requests, reviews, and records held through a booking platform. The company says it has not confirmed electronic medical records were taken, and the categories overlap — don't add them up. Change reused passwords; be wary of messages using your appointment details.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT:]** Company disclosure · ~1.62M people (basic info) · EMR acquisition not confirmed

### Signal 5 — Wearables and exercise (7:50–8:40)

**NARRATION**
A University of Mississippi analysis of a national US survey found about one in three wearable-focused or multi-tool users met both aerobic and strength guidelines, versus about one in five who mainly used portals or engaged little. The researchers say it doesn't prove the devices cause more activity — active people may just be likelier to buy them.
