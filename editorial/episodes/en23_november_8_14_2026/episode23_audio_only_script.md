## Under the Microscope

On the tenth of November, the Federal Trade Commission announced that five more states — Arizona, Florida, Nebraska, Ohio, and Wisconsin — had joined its lawsuit against Hims and Hers. California and Utah were already part of the case, filed in July.

Let's be precise about what this is. It's a lawsuit. Everything I'm about to describe is an allegation in the amended complaint. No court has ruled, and Hims and Hers has said it will vigorously defend itself.

So what do the regulators say?

First, the promises. According to the complaint, the company told customers that medical records and sensitive information are only accessed by the medical providers managing their care. It described a "one hundred percent online, private, and secure process."

Second, what allegedly happened instead. The complaint says Hims and Hers uploaded about fourteen million customer email addresses to Meta between 2020 and 2023 to build advertising audiences. About ten million matched Meta users. And the audience names are telling. One was called "All Time Hims E-D Purchasers." Another, "Hers Apollo Signups" — and Apollo, the complaint says, was an internal codeword for mental health.

The regulators also allege that sign-up and purchase events were sent to Meta about eight million times, tagged by treatment category. And that email lists went to Snap too — including one named "All Time Hers Psych Signups."

Here's the important nuance. This is not a hack. The complaint doesn't say medical records leaked. It says email addresses, and labels showing what kind of treatment someone signed up for, reached advertising companies — despite the privacy promises.

There's a second part to the case: billing. The complaint describes a checkout button that said "Pay zero dollars today," with auto-renewal terms in small, faint text. It alleges refills were charged early, and that the cancel option was labelled "Add or remove items from order," followed by several survey screens.

Why does this matter?

Because "private" can mean two different things. A company can lock away your clinical notes and still — if these allegations are right — let ad systems learn what you were treated for. For many people, that is the sensitive part.

Last week, we looked at hospital websites and the Meta Pixel. This week, regulators are suing a health company itself, under consumer-protection law — not HIPAA.

And this does not mean telehealth is unsafe in general. It's one case, still to be decided.

So what can you do? Pause on this for a moment.

Read the privacy policy, not just the banner, and look for the words "advertising partners." Consider a separate email address for health sign-ups. Before you pay, check the renewal date and the full price after any "zero dollars today" offer. And ask any health app one question: which companies learn what I buy or sign up for?

Looking five to ten years out, more care will start in an app than in a waiting room. Regulators are signalling that what you were treated for deserves the same protection as your medical record itself.

## This Week's Signals

Now, four signals.

Signal two: a smartwatch heart test that improved when people repeated it.

At the American Heart Association's Scientific Sessions, researchers presented an AI model that reads thirty-second smartwatch ECGs for signs of weak heart pumping. This is a conference abstract — not yet peer-reviewed — from one hospital in South Korea, in people already referred for a heart scan.

Over two thousand adults recorded ECGs on both an Apple Watch and a Galaxy Watch. The AI performed similarly on both, but nine percent of people got different results on the two watches. And at home, requiring a positive result on two of three days cut false alarms in half — from eighteen to nine out of seventy people — while catching nearly as many real cases. One more thing: the presenter is chief medical officer of the company that built the AI.

This is not a feature on your watch. But the lesson is worth keeping: patterns over days may beat single alerts.

Signal three: AI-drafted heart-ultrasound reports.

Kaiser Permanente Northern California ran a randomized, blinded study, also presented as a conference abstract. AI, sonographers, and cardiologists each drafted preliminary reports on twelve hundred echocardiograms. Cardiologists made substantial edits to about half of the AI drafts — and about half of the human drafts. That met the study's test for "non-inferior" — in plain words, AI drafts needed fixing about as often. Cardiologists' reading time dropped from eight and a half minutes to under five.

And crucially, cardiologists reviewed and signed every report. The study didn't measure patient outcomes. The question to ask in the future isn't whether AI wrote the draft — it's who checked it.

Signal four: chatbots and drug ads.

A peer-reviewed experiment in The Lancet Digital Health, from Mount Sinai, tested twelve consumer AI models on thirty-three simulated cases — with and without a clearly labelled drug ad. With the ad present, the models chose the advertised drug about forty-eight percent of the time, versus thirty-five percent without. In four cases, it jumped from never to always.

The reassuring part: the ads didn't clearly push models toward unproven supplements or clearly worse drugs. And no real patients were involved. But if you ask a chatbot about treatment, treat its answer as a question for your clinician — not a recommendation.

Signal five: sleep regularity and mood.

A peer-reviewed study in Scientific Reports used smart-ring data from nearly twenty-three thousand people. Irregular sleep was associated with more self-reported depression and anxiety symptoms. Associated — not caused. We don't know which comes first. And the study was funded in part by the ring maker, Oura, with some authors tied to the company.

A steady sleep schedule is a sensible habit. But a ring score is not a mental-health assessment. If you're worried about your mood, please talk to a qualified professional.

## Global Watch

Global Watch takes us to India.

On the fourteenth of November, India's technology ministry opened registration for "consent managers" under its national data-protection law. These are registered companies that give people one place to give, manage, and withdraw consent for how their data is used — and they must not be able to read that data themselves. Most of the law's other duties, including people's data rights, follow around May 2027.

These consent managers are separate from the one in India's digital health mission. But the model is worth watching: consent, managed by an accountable intermediary, on your behalf.

## Privacy Radar

Time for the Privacy Radar.

Hims and Hers: high risk. Treatment categories linked to email addresses allegedly reached ad platforms. Your action: look for "advertising partners" in privacy policies, and opt out of targeted ads.

Smartwatch ECG: medium. Heart data sits with watch makers and AI developers. Your action: check what your watch app shares — and treat any flag as a reason to see a clinician.

AI-drafted echo reports: low. The data stayed inside the health system. Your action: it's fair to ask whether AI helped with your report, and who reviewed it.

Chatbots and drug ads: medium. Chatbot providers hold your conversations. Your action: avoid sharing identifying health details, and check data settings.

Smart-ring sleep: medium. Months of sleep data sit with the ring maker. Your action: review sharing and research-consent settings.
