## 3. 1:10–4:40 — Under the Microscope

### 3a. What happened (1:10–2:20)

**NARRATION**
On 10 November, the FTC announced that Arizona, Florida, Nebraska, Ohio and Wisconsin had joined its lawsuit against Hims & Hers, alongside California and Utah. The original case was filed in July.

This is an enforcement lawsuit. Everything here is an allegation in the amended complaint. No court has ruled, and Hims & Hers says it will vigorously defend itself.

According to the complaint, the company promised that medical records and sensitive information are only accessed by the providers managing your care, and advertised a "100% online, private, and secure process."

### 3b. What the regulators allege (2:20–3:10)

**NARRATION**
Here's what they say happened. About 14 million customer email addresses uploaded to Meta between 2020 and 2023 to build ad audiences — about 10 million matched. Audience names like "All Time Hims ED Purchasers" and "Hers Apollo Signups" — Apollo, the complaint says, was an internal codeword for mental health. Sign-up and purchase events sent to Meta about 8 million times, tagged by treatment category. Email lists shared with Snap.

And here's the nuance: this is not a hack, and the complaint doesn't say medical records leaked. It says email addresses and treatment labels reached advertising companies, despite the privacy promise.

## 4. 4:40–8:20 — This Week's Signals

### Signal 2 — Smartwatch AI-ECG (4:40–5:40)

**NARRATION**
At the American Heart Association's Scientific Sessions, researchers presented an AI that reads 30-second smartwatch ECGs for signs of weak heart pumping. Status line first: a conference abstract, not peer-reviewed, from one South Korean hospital, in people already referred for a heart scan.

Over 2,200 adults used both an Apple Watch and a Galaxy Watch. The AI did similarly on both — but 9% got different results on the two devices. At home, requiring a positive on two of three days cut false alarms from 18 to 9 of 70 people, while catching 31 of 38 real cases instead of 33. The presenter is chief medical officer of the company behind the AI.

This isn't a watch feature, and no watch result replaces an echocardiogram. The lesson: patterns over days may beat single alerts.

### Signal 3 — AI-drafted echo reports (5:40–6:40)

**NARRATION**
Kaiser Permanente Northern California ran a randomized, blinded study, also a conference abstract. AI, sonographers and cardiologists drafted preliminary reports on 1,200 heart ultrasounds from 21 centres. Cardiologists made substantial edits to 50.6% of AI drafts and 49.7% of human drafts — "non-inferior," meaning about as often. Reading time fell from 8.5 to 4.6 minutes.

Cardiologists reviewed and finalised every report, and the study didn't measure patient outcomes. The future question isn't whether AI wrote it, but who checked it.

**[VISUAL CUE:]** Two stacked report cards, "AI draft" and "Human draft", each receiving amber edit marks at nearly the same rate; a clinician-signature line (abstract, no face) closes both in teal. Clock icon 8.5 → 4.6 min.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT:]** "Randomized, blinded · conference abstract" · "Edits: 50.6% AI vs 49.7% human" · "Every report signed by a cardiologist"

### Signal 4 — Drug ads and AI chatbots (6:40–7:30)

**NARRATION**
A peer-reviewed controlled experiment in The Lancet Digital Health, from Mount Sinai, tested 12 consumer AI models on 33 simulated cases, with or without a clearly labelled drug ad. With the ad, models picked the advertised drug 47.6% of the time, versus 35% without — and in four cases, from 0% to 100%.

Ads didn't clearly push models toward unproven supplements or clearly worse drugs, and no real patients were involved. Still: treat chatbot answers as questions for your clinician.

### Signal 5 — Smart-ring sleep regularity (7:30–8:20)

**NARRATION**
A peer-reviewed study in Scientific Reports used smart-ring data from nearly 23,000 people. Irregular sleep was associated with more self-reported depression and anxiety symptoms. Associated — not caused; we don't know which comes first. It was funded in part by Oura, and some authors have ties to the company.

A ring score is not a mental-health assessment. If you're worried about your mood, talk to a qualified professional.

## Source and evidence safeguards

- **Hims & Hers:** always "the FTC and seven states allege" / "according to the complaint". Never "Hims sold/leaked data", "breach", or "violated HIPAA". Do not claim the emails were or were not hashed. Mention that Hims says it will defend itself. No real Hims, Meta or Snap logos or screens.
- **Smartwatch ECG:** "flagged", never "diagnosed"; always pair with "people already referred for a heart scan at one Korean hospital"; disclose the presenter's company role; never imply the feature is on any watch.
- **Echo AI:** "non-inferior" = "needed edits about as often"; cardiologists finalised every report; no claim of better care or outcomes.
- **Drug ads:** "controlled experiment", "simulated cases"; no individual chatbot named as biased; state that ads did not clearly push unsafe picks.
- **Sleep:** "associated with"; never causal; disclose Oura funding; include the gentle professional-support line; no mental-health advice.
- **Global Watch:** "registration opened"; rights and penalties "from around May 2027"; do not conflate with ABDM.
- Journal pages for the Lancet Digital Health and Scientific Reports papers were not opened during production; figures come from institutional releases and specialist coverage reproducing the abstracts.
